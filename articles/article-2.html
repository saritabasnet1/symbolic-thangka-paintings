Wheel of Life in Symbolic Thangka: Reading the Bhavachakra Step by Step

In short: the Wheel of Life, or Bhavachakra, is a circular teaching painting that shows the round of existence in one image. It explains karma and rebirth through its rings: the centre shows the causes that keep the wheel turning, the middle rings show different conditions of life, and the figure outside the wheel points to the path out of suffering.

The Wheel of Life is the best-known symbolic thangka subject, and a useful place to learn how to read a symbolic painting. Start at the centre and work outwards, ring by ring, and the teaching builds up in the same order.

What Is the Wheel of Life (Bhavachakra)?

The Bhavachakra is a wheel held by a large figure, with the whole round of existence shown inside it. The word Bhavachakra means wheel of becoming, and the image is a picture of that process. Beings move through different conditions, driven by causes, and the wheel turns as long as those causes continue. The painting does not present this as a story with a single hero. It presents it as a pattern that can be studied, ring by ring.

As a symbolic thangka, the Wheel of Life is a teaching painting, not a single-deity portrait. It uses many small figures across registers and landscapes, each gesture following scriptural description, and it is painted in mineral colour with gold detail on cotton canvas. It is hung in monasteries, study rooms and home shrines. For an introduction, see Symbolic Thangka Paintings: Meaning, History and Buddhist Teaching.

How Should You Begin Reading It?

Begin at the centre and move outwards. The centre explains why the wheel turns, and the outer rings show what that turning looks like. Read the hub first, then the ring beside it, then the six realms, then the outer rim, and finish with the figure outside the wheel.

What Is at the Centre of the Wheel?

At the centre is a small circle, the hub, holding three animals chasing one another in a circle. In the traditional reading, they stand for three causes: greed, hatred and delusion. Each animal bites the tail of the one before it, so the circle has no beginning and no end. That is the point of the image. As long as these three causes are present, the wheel keeps turning, and beings keep moving through different conditions.

What Does the Second Ring Show?

Beside the hub is a ring divided into light and dark halves. One half shows figures moving upwards, and the other shows figures moving downwards. In the traditional reading, this ring shows karma in action: actions that lead towards a better condition, and actions that lead towards a worse one. The division is simple, and that simplicity is useful for teaching. It gives a clear picture of a basic idea before the painting moves on to detail.

What Are the Six Realms?

The next ring is divided into six sections, each showing a different realm, or condition of existence. The six realms are a standard part of this teaching, and each one shows beings in a different condition, with different experiences and different difficulties. The painting does not present them as places on a map. It presents them as conditions that beings move through, driven by the causes shown at the centre.

Read the six sections in order, and notice the differences between them. One section shows beings in comfort, another shows beings in conflict, another shows beings in hunger or thirst. The differences are the point. No condition is shown as lasting, and no condition is shown as separate from the causes that lead to it. The ring teaches by contrast: each realm is different, yet each is part of the same wheel.

What Does the Outer Rim Show?

The outer rim is divided into twelve sections, each showing a link in a chain. In the traditional reading, these are the twelve links of dependent arising, a chain that explains how one condition leads to the next, from ignorance through to old age and death. Each section shows a small scene, and the scenes follow one another around the rim in order, so the viewer can follow the chain step by step.

Who Holds the Wheel, and What Is Outside It?

The wheel itself is held by a large figure, shown holding the rim from outside. That figure is part of the teaching, not a decoration. It shows that the wheel is held, and that it can be seen as a whole. The figure’s size and position make the wheel the centre of attention, while the figure itself frames it. A viewer who notices the holder understands that the wheel is being presented for study, not presented as a world without an edge.

Outside the wheel, at the top, is a figure pointing towards a pale circle, often read as the moon. That circle stands for the path out of the wheel. The painting does not end with the wheel turning. It ends with a direction: the causes can be understood, and there is a path out of suffering.

How Do Teachers Use the Wheel of Life?

Teachers use the Wheel of Life by reading it in order, ring by ring, as described above. A teacher can point to the hub and explain the three causes, then point to the second ring and explain karma, then move through the six realms and the outer rim. At each step, the painting gives the student something to look at while the teacher speaks, and the student can return to the painting later and repeat the reading alone.

The painting is hung where it can be studied, in a monastery, a study room or a home shrine. A Wheel of Life painting, semi-antique in style, on canvas, 50 by 70 centimetres, is large enough for its small scenes to be seen clearly. The same reading skill transfers to other symbolic subjects. The Life of Buddha Thangka: How a Painting Tells the Buddha's Story uses a different order — a life told scene by scene — and Jataka Tales in Thangka Art: Stories, Symbols and Moral Lessons uses short stories in small scenes. Both reward the same slow, ordered looking that the Wheel of Life teaches first.

Conclusion

The Wheel of Life is a circular lesson. Its hub shows the causes that keep it turning, its rings show the results of those causes, its six realms show different conditions, its outer rim shows a chain of twelve links, and the figure outside it points to a path out. Read from the centre outwards, it explains karma, rebirth and the path out of suffering in a single image.

Learn the order, and the painting becomes a tool you can use again and again. Start at the hub, follow each ring in turn, and finish outside the wheel. That is the reading the painting was made for, and it is the reading that makes a symbolic thangka more than a picture.
